# Public Product Study Method

Use this method when the user wants to learn from an existing product or brings one in as evidence for a new direction. The goal is to explain why the product may work, not to produce a feature inventory or a company biography.

Read `lawful-translation-boundaries.md` first. Study only lawfully accessible material and keep facts separate from causal interpretation.

## Three valid starting points

The user may provide:

1. **A target problem only.** Research source products after the target structure is clear.
2. **A source product only.** Build the dossier and identify candidate mechanisms. Do not invent a target market or claim transferability until the user supplies a target or asks for exploratory directions.
3. **A source product and a target problem.** Study the source independently before testing whether its mechanism maps to the target.

Keep the two sides distinct. Evidence that a source product exists or appears successful does not prove that the target problem is real.

## Evidence discipline

Every material statement in the dossier needs either a direct citation or an explicit evidence label:

- **Observed public fact:** directly visible through ordinary lawful use or public material.
- **Publicly sourced claim:** attributed and linked to an identifiable public source.
- **Inference:** the analyst's explanation, with its evidence, uncertainty, and a competing explanation.
- **Unverified lead:** worth checking, but not evidence and not sufficient for a `YES` or `BUILD` conclusion.

Prefer sources in this rough order for the claim they can actually support:

1. official product documentation, pricing, terms, filings, public datasets, and original research;
2. direct lawful observation of the public product experience;
3. published interviews and credible independent reporting;
4. public user reviews and community discussion.

No source type proves everything. Official material can establish product behaviour but may overstate impact; reviews can reveal recurring friction but not prevalence; interviews can explain intent but not causation. Triangulate important claims and record disagreement.

## Source Product Dossier: six lenses

### 1. User and context

Identify the primary actor, the moment that triggers use, the job they are trying to complete, the previous behaviour, and the constraints around the decision. Distinguish the direct user from the buyer, beneficiary, supplier, administrator, or regulated party when they differ.

The useful question is not “who is the product for?” but “who changes behaviour, in what moment, and under which constraint?”

### 2. Value proposition

Describe the changed outcome in the user's terms. Name which cost is reduced or which capability is gained: time, uncertainty, effort, risk, coordination, access, status, or money. Compare it with the user's actual alternative, including doing nothing or using a manual workaround.

Avoid repeating the source product's marketing slogan as analysis.

### 3. Key mechanism

Separate the visible product surface from the causal mechanism underneath it:

| Surface feature or step | Immediate user/system effect | Inferred mechanism | Evidence label and source | Competing explanation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What can be seen | What changes immediately | Why that change may affect behaviour or system state | Fact, sourced claim, inference, or unverified lead | What else might explain the result |

Express the strongest mechanism as:

`starting structure → intervention → changed behaviour or system state → outcome`

A feature such as a streak, rating, template, feed, membership fee, or approval flow is not yet a mechanism. Explain what it changes: salience, commitment, trust, search cost, incentives, coordination, identity, feedback speed, or another structural variable.

### 4. Business model and incentives

Map who creates value, who receives value, who pays, what is subsidised, and which behaviour the revenue model rewards. Note likely cost drivers and conflicts between user value and value capture.

Do not assume a mechanism transfers when the target has a different payer, margin structure, transaction frequency, regulatory burden, or participant incentive.

### 5. Growth and retention

Distinguish acquisition from repeat value. Identify the observable or claimed loop that may bring users in, help the product improve, or make users return. Examples include distribution partnerships, contribution loops, reputation accumulation, saved state, switching cost, habit, network liquidity, and recurring workflow value.

Treat “virality”, “network effects”, and “community” as hypotheses until the reinforcing loop and its evidence are shown. Also ask whether brand, timing, capital, bundling, or privileged distribution is a more plausible explanation than the product mechanism.

### 6. Constraints and failure boundary

Record the conditions that make the source mechanism possible: trust baseline, market density, transaction value, frequency, regulation, data availability, user motivation, supply quality, cultural norms, operational labour, or distribution access.

Name the earliest condition under which the mechanism stops producing the intended outcome. This boundary matters more for translation than a long list of source-product strengths.

## From dossier to translation

Do not move directly from the dossier to a copied feature set. Use this sequence:

1. **Strip the surface.** Remove the brand, industry nouns, feature names, interface labels, and visual expression.
2. **State the source structure.** Describe the original actors, frictions, incentives, information conditions, and constraints.
3. **Name the mechanism.** Show the causal chain and its evidence status.
4. **Test a rival explanation.** Ask whether distribution, brand, timing, regulation, capital, or another mechanism better explains the observed outcome.
5. **Build a Target-Context Translation Map.** Map structural variables one by one rather than relying on overall resemblance.
6. **Stress-test the transfer.** Identify what does not map, the critical difference, the failure condition, and a result that would falsify the translation.

## Completion standard

A dossier is ready for transfer testing only when it:

- covers all six lenses without disguising unknowns;
- cites or labels every material claim;
- distinguishes surface features from at least one causal mechanism;
- includes a competing explanation;
- identifies enabling constraints and a failure boundary;
- keeps source-product evidence separate from target-problem evidence.

If these conditions are not met, retain the case as an `Unverified lead` or keep the thesis at `PARK` and name the missing research.
